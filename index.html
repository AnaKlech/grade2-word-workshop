Words that change

Two lessons about words that break the usual rules. About 25 minutes. Get your paper and a pencil before you start.

1

Irregular plurals

Plural means more than one.

One cat → two cats. One book → five books. Most of the time you just add s.

But some words do not want an s. They change in a funny way, or they do not change at all. We call these irregular plurals. There is no trick for them — you just learn them by heart, like the names of your friends.

These words change inside

oneman→men
onewoman→women
onechild→children
oneperson→people
onefoot→feet
onetooth→teeth
onegoose→geese
onemouse→mice

These words stay exactly the same

onesheep→sheep
onefish→fish
onedeer→deer
Write this down
  1. At the top of your paper write: Plural means more than one.
  2. Make two columns: One and Many.
  3. Copy all 11 word pairs into your columns. Say each pair out loud as you write it — “one foot, two feet.”
  4. Put a star next to the three words that did not change.

Now you try — 11 questions

2

Past tense verbs

Past tense means it already happened.

Today I play → yesterday I played. Today I jump → yesterday I jumped. Most of the time you just add ed.

But some verbs are irregular. They do not take ed — they turn into a whole new word. You cannot say “I swimmed.” You have to say “I swam.” Here are 14 of them to keep in your head.

Group A — only the middle sound changes

swim→swam
run→ran
drink→drank
sing→sang
give→gave
take→took
write→wrote

Group B — the whole word changes

catch→caught
throw→threw
fly→flew
see→saw
eat→ate
sleep→slept
go→went
Write this down
  1. At the top of your paper write: Past tense means it already happened.
  2. Make two columns — two lists side by side: Today and Yesterday.
  3. Copy Group A into your columns. Then stand up and stretch.
  4. Now copy Group B.
  5. Pick your three favourite verbs and write a sentence for each that starts with Yesterday I…

Now you try — 14 questions

Describing & building words

Two lessons about words that tell us more, then a fun one about words made out of other words. About 30 minutes. Take a stretch break after Lesson 4.

3

Adjectives

An adjective describes a noun.

A noun is a person, a place, or a thing. The adjective tells you what that noun is like.

Here is your secret tool. To find the adjective, point at the noun and ask:

“What kind?”

The word that answers your question is the adjective. (Sometimes you can also ask How many? or Which one?)

Watch the tool work:

  • The red balloon flew away.

    What kind of balloon? → a red one. So red is the adjective.

  • I have a fluffy cat.

    What kind of cat? → a fluffy one. So fluffy is the adjective.

  • She read a funny book.

    What kind of book? → a funny one. So funny is the adjective.

  • My brother is tall.

    What kind of brother? → a tall one. So tall is the adjective.

  • Three noisy birds sat on the fence.

    What kind of birds? → noisy. How many birds? → three. This sentence has two adjectives!

Write this down
  1. Write: An adjective describes a noun. It tells what kind.
  2. Under it, draw a box and write your tool inside: My question: What kind?
  3. Copy two of the examples above and circle the adjective in each one.

4

Adverbs

An adverb describes a verb.

A verb is an action word — run, sing, eat, walk. The adverb tells you more about that action. Lots of adverbs end in –ly.

Here is your tool for adverbs. Point at the action and ask:

“How?”

The word that answers your question is the adverb. It can sit at the front, in the middle, or at the end of the sentence — so find the action word first, then ask it How? Never just grab the last word. (Sometimes you can also ask When? or Where?)

Watch the tool work:

  • The turtle walked slowly.

    How did the turtle walk? → slowly. So slowly is the adverb.

  • My dad sings loudly in the car.

    How does he sing? → loudly. So loudly is the adverb.

  • He quietly closed the door.

    How did he close it? → quietly. So quietly is the adverb.

  • Slowly the snail climbed up the wall.

    How did the snail climb? → slowly. So slowly is the adverb.

  • We will go to the zoo tomorrow.

    When will we go? → tomorrow. This adverb tells the time.

  • The dog sleeps outside every night.

    Where does the dog sleep? → outside. This adverb tells the place.

Careful! Do not mix them up.

The quick fox ran quickly. Quick tells about the fox — a noun, so it is an adjective. Quickly tells about the running — a verb, so it is an adverb.

Write this down
  1. Write: An adverb describes a verb. It tells how.
  2. Draw a box and write your tool inside: My question: How?
  3. Copy two of the examples above and circle the adverb in each one.
  4. At the bottom write the careful part: Adjective → noun. Adverb → verb.

Find the adverb — 8 questions

Compound words

A compound word is two small words joined into one big word.

sun + shine = sunshine. Both little words are still hiding inside the big one.

This is the friendliest rule in the whole workshop. Nothing changes and nothing gets added — you just push two words together and a brand new word pops out. Read each one below and see if you can spot the two little words before you look.

Two little words → one big word

sun+shine=sunshine
rain+bow=rainbow
butter+fly=butterfly
cup+cake=cupcake
snow+man=snowman
tooth+brush=toothbrush
bed+room=bedroom
foot+ball=football
back+pack=backpack
star+fish=starfish
play+ground=playground
birth+day=birthday
pop+corn=popcorn
sand+castle=sandcastle
pan+cake=pancake
Careful! Not every one means what it looks like.

A butterfly is not a fly made of butter. Sometimes the two little words join up and the big word means something all of its own.

You have met some of these little words already. Tooth from Lesson 1 is hiding in toothbrush, foot is hiding in football, and fish is hiding in starfish.

Write this down
  1. Write: A compound word is two small words joined into one big word.
  2. Under it write your example with the signs: sun + shine = sunshine
  3. Pick five more words from the list and write them out the same way.
  4. Now draw one of them. Can somebody at home guess which compound word you drew?

Letters & review

One short lesson, then four questions from every topic. About 25 minutes. Keep your paper next to you — you are allowed to look at it during the review.

6

The comma in a letter greeting

When you write a letter, it has parts that always go in the same order. Here is a whole letter. Look closely at the very first line.

Greeting Dear Grandma,
Body Thank you for the blue socks. I wore them to school and my teacher liked them.
Closing Love,
Your name Mila
Greeting = Dear + name + comma.

The comma goes after the name, never after the word Dear. The name always starts with a capital letter.

Good greetings look like this:

Dear Mom,   Dear Mr. Lee,   Dear Anna,   Dear Uncle Tom,

The closing at the end gets a comma too — Love, or Your friend, — but today we are practising the beginning.

Write this down
  1. Write this greeting on your paper and make the comma big and dark: Dear Grandma,
  2. Under it write: The comma goes after the name.
  3. Now write three greetings for three real people you know.

Now you try — 5 questions

0 of 5 correct
